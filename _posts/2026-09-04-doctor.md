## Abstract
Equity-aligned efforts have been crucial to helping address current and historic injustices across policies, systems, and institutions in the U.S. At the same time, some of these efforts have resulted in little to no progress, and anything relating to equity has recently become a target for dismantlement. Within this dissertation, I argue that good intentions alone are not enough when it comes to equity— and that poor implementations of well-intentioned efforts may in fact hinder our progress towards equity. Specifically, I analyze an NSF-based effort within computing. Within computing, the homogeneity in the demographics of the field has long been a topic of concern, especially in academia. As such, efforts to ‘broaden participation’ in computing (BPC) have sought to reduce disparities across populations considered ‘underrepresented’ in the field of computer science (CS). One of these efforts came from the National Science Foundation (NSF), who in 2017 began requiring investigators to include two-page documents or ‘plans’ outlining how their departments were working towards BPC. Drawing on the scholarship of Drs. Moya Bailey, Talila A. Lewis, and Sami Schalk, I use ableism as a lens through which to analyze 128 publicly (at the time) available BPC Plans and answer the following questions: 

RQ 1) How do departments conceptualize accessibility and disability within efforts to broaden participation in computing? 

RQ 2) In what ways are departments’ efforts to ‘broaden participation in computing’ disabling? In what ways are they enabling? 

I find that, despite people with disabilities being one of the ‘underrepresented’ demographics in computing, most plans fail to include us or pay attention to accessibility. Additionally, despite the good intentions of departments, many of the efforts they describe reinforce ableist values and beliefs, and as such, may be counterproductive and cause more harm than good with regard to broadening participation. Importantly, most plans fail to consider or address the systemic forces impacting the continued exclusion of minoritized populations, which I argue is not only a hindrance to their success but also a contributor to the ease with which they have been dismantled amidst administration changes.
